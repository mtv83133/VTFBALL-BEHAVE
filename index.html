DISC Behavioral Profiling

The DISC model is one of the most widely validated behavioral frameworks in sports and organizational psychology, used by professional teams and Fortune 500 companies. It measures four core dimensions — Dominance, Influence, Steadiness, Correctness — and maps directly to how an athlete performs, communicates, and leads under pressure.

Identifies dominant behavioral style across 4 dimensions
Surfaces each athlete's dominant fear — the root of avoidance and stress behavior
Position-weighted scoring calibrates results by role (QB scores differently than OL)
Forced-choice question format minimizes social desirability bias

Focus Domain Assessment

Five cognitive and interpersonal domains specifically relevant to football performance: Flexibility (scheme adaptation), Organization (preparation quality), Communication (in-game clarity), Understanding (team attunement), and Stability (pressure response). Each scored 0–100 with sub-facet granularity.

Predicts scheme installation speed and adaptability to new coordinators
Stability score is a direct predictor of bounce-back ability after turnovers or injuries
Communication score flags athletes who won't self-report issues — a retention risk
Understanding score identifies natural locker room leaders and team culture anchors

Coach FAQ

QUESTIONS
COACHES ASK.

Every question your staff has asked — answered honestly.

How accurate is this, really? Can't athletes just say what they think we want to hear? +

This is the most important question. Standard self-report surveys are easily gamed — athletes answer to look coachable. Gridiron Profile uses a forced-choice scenario format, meaning athletes must choose between options that all appear socially acceptable. There's no obvious "right" answer. This is the same methodology used in professional sports and military officer selection because it's far more resistant to impression management than scale-based surveys.

That said, no behavioral assessment is a lie detector. The most accurate results come when athletes are told honestly: "This helps us coach you better. There are no wrong answers. Trying to game it just gives us bad data and worse coaching." Framing matters. When athletes trust the purpose, the data is significantly more reliable.

How is this different from the Wonderlic or other football tests? +

The Wonderlic measures cognitive processing speed — essentially, how fast an athlete can solve problems under time pressure. It tells you nothing about how they respond to adversity, communicate with teammates, or stay motivated through a losing streak. It also has a documented racial bias in norms and has been largely abandoned by NFL teams as a primary evaluation tool.

Gridiron Profile measures behavioral style, emotional resilience, communication patterns, and motivational values — the things that predict locker room culture, transfer retention, and how an athlete responds when they're down 14 in the fourth quarter. These are orthogonal capabilities. A high-Wonderlic athlete can still be a locker room problem. A lower-Wonderlic athlete can be your most dependable cultural anchor.

Is there legal or compliance risk in using this for scholarship or roster decisions? +

Behavioral assessments used as one input among many in athletic program decisions carry minimal legal risk, particularly when they measure voluntary behavioral tendencies rather than protected psychological conditions. This platform does not screen for mental health diagnoses, disabilities, or any characteristic protected under ADA or Title IX.

Best practice — which this platform is designed to support — is to use the results as a coaching and development tool, not a binary gate. The platform generates coaching guidance, not pass/fail scores. Scholarship decisions should always be multi-factor, with behavioral data informing development plans rather than overriding talent evaluations. When used this way, programs at the professional and collegiate level have used DISC-based tools for decades without legal challenge.

What does the DISC score actually tell me about an athlete on the field? +

High D (Dominance): This athlete makes fast, assertive decisions. Great for positions requiring split-second leadership — QB, MIKE linebacker, SS. Risk: may override play calls under pressure or create friction with authority.

High I (Influence): People-driven, energizing presence. Natural hype captain. Can lose focus during repetitive installation or when the environment is low-energy. Needs visibility and social connection to stay engaged.

High S (Steadiness): Consistent, dependable, process-oriented. The most reliable execution athlete on your roster. Struggles with rapid scheme changes or unclear roles. Needs stability and trust-based relationships with coaches.

High C (Correctness): Detail-oriented, analytical, self-critical. Exceptional at film study and technical mastery. Under pressure, overthinks and becomes hypercritical of their own mistakes. Needs precise, evidence-based feedback — not vague encouragement.

How do we use this in the transfer portal specifically? +

Send the assessment link to any transfer prospect as part of your standard intake process — before the official visit, during it, or after. The athlete completes it in 15 minutes on their phone. You receive a full profile immediately.

Key things to look for in portal evaluations: Flexibility score (predicts how fast they'll absorb your scheme), Stability score (how they'll handle the emotional disruption of transferring), Core Values alignment (does what drives them match what your program offers?), and Communication score (will they self-report problems or bottle things up?). A talented player who scores low on Flexibility and whose top value is Freedom coming into a highly structured system is a known risk — now you can see it before you sign.

Will our players actually take this seriously and answer honestly? +

Compliance and honesty are highest when athletes understand the purpose and believe the results will be used for their benefit. Frame it as a development tool, not a screening tool. Tell them: "This helps your position coach understand how to communicate with you and develop you. The more honest you are, the better we can help you."

For incoming recruits or transfers, frame it as: "We use this to make sure you get the right coaching approach from day one — not a one-size-fits-all system." Athletes who've been in programs that ignored their individual needs respond very well to this framing. Consider having a trusted team leader vouch for the process — a respected veteran explaining how it helped their development goes further than any staff announcement.

How often should athletes retake the assessment? +

Core behavioral style (DISC) is relatively stable over time — it reflects deeply ingrained tendencies that don't shift dramatically year to year. We recommend an initial assessment on entry, then a reassessment every 12–18 months, or after a significant life event (major injury, personal crisis, change in family circumstances).

Focus domain scores are more responsive to development and coaching — an athlete who works with a sports psychologist on mental resilience may show meaningful Stability score improvement in 6–9 months. Core Values tend to shift most during major life stage transitions — sophomore-to-junior year is often when "Recognition" drops and "Advancement" or "Security" rises as NFL consideration becomes real. Tracking these shifts is one of the most valuable long-term uses of the platform.

What's the biggest mistake programs make when using behavioral tools? +

Three patterns show up consistently. First: using it as a gate, not a guide. Eliminating a recruit because their Stability score is low misses the point — that score tells you what kind of support they need, not that they can't play. The best use is developing a coaching plan around the data, not screening people out.

Second: reading the data without context. A High D athlete isn't "difficult" — they're decisive. A Low Communication athlete isn't "secretive" — they process internally. Every score needs to be interpreted through the lens of the role and the situation, not as a character judgment.

Third: not sharing the data with the athlete. The biggest ROI comes when athletes see their own profile and understand themselves better. Self-awareness is coachable. An athlete who understands that their Low Stability means they ruminate over mistakes can work with a sports psychologist to build reset protocols. The data is most powerful when it's a shared language between coach and athlete, not just a staff-only report.

Can we add our own questions or customize the assessment for our program? +

Yes — this is one of the core advantages of an exclusive platform over an off-the-shelf SaaS tool. Custom questions can be added for program-specific priorities: academic accountability, NIL responsibilities, social media conduct, position-group specific scenarios, or any other behavioral dimension your staff wants to track. Custom questions appear as a fourth section of the assessment and their scores feed directly into updated staff flags and coaching action plans. Because this platform belongs to your program, the question bank evolves as your needs evolve.

How do we interpret results for a player we're considering cutting or redshirting? +

Behavioral data should inform these conversations, not replace them. For a potential redshirt, the most relevant scores are Stability (will they handle the redshirt year emotionally without transferring?), Core Values (is long-term development a driver, or do they need immediate playing time to stay engaged?), and Flexibility (will they embrace an expanded role concept?).

For roster management decisions, the behavioral profile is most useful in the conversation itself. Knowing that an athlete's top value is Family/Loyalty means the conversation should start with relationship — acknowledge what they've contributed, make them feel seen — before delivering hard news. Knowing their top value is Advancement means you should lead with the opportunity framing: "A year of development positions you better for what comes next." The data makes difficult conversations more effective, not unnecessary.
